---
title: Hosts
description: What makes a Mac and a Linux PC behave well as the machine being watched.
---

Any machine running Sunshine can be watched. The two below are the ones this was built and measured on, and what each needed. The scripts named here live in [eduwass/dotfiles](https://github.com/eduwass/dotfiles) under `infra/kvm`.

## A Mac

Sunshine on macOS needs three things to feel like a local desktop.

- **A display that follows the window.** `vdisplay` (in `mac-host/`) makes a virtual display and sets it to whatever size the stream asks for, at 2x, so a resized window gets a sharp picture and not a scaled one.
- **A build that keeps up.** Sunshine's ScreenCaptureKit capture, with a small patch (`mac-host/sunshine-local.patch`): the encoder's "real time" flag off (14 ms a frame at 3400 × 1912 instead of 17.5), and capture paced to the stream's rate.
- **Awake displays.** A Mac's displays sleep after a few idle minutes, and Sunshine then refuses a stream: "Failed to initialize video capture/encoding. Is a display connected and turned on?" Wake them first. As the device's **Before connecting** command:

```sh
ssh my-mac 'caffeinate -u -t 30 >/dev/null 2>&1 & sleep 1'
```

For the **instant pointer**, the host runs `cursor-share` (in `mac-host/`), which sends the cursor's shape to your Mac and tells Sunshine to leave its own cursor out of the picture. It gets to your Mac in one of two ways. If the device has an ssh destination in its **Clipboard** field, your Mac starts the helper over ssh and reads the cursor from there; nothing on the host has to be allowed, and the shapes are there a tenth of a second after the first picture. Without one, the host's prep command starts the helper and it connects to your Mac: macOS on the host then asks once whether "sunshine" may find devices on the local network (and again after a rebuilt Sunshine), and until that is allowed you see the host's own cursor, one round trip behind.

On the Mac, in this order: `mac-host/install.sh` builds the helpers and sets up the services, `mac-host/build-sunshine.sh` builds Sunshine with the local patch, and `mac-host/sunshine-install-signed.sh`, in a terminal on that Mac, signs it and walks you through the approvals macOS asks for. `mac-host/doctor.sh` checks all of this from another machine.

When a stream ends, the host is made quiet: while it was streamed Sunshine had its sound, and what was playing would otherwise carry on out loud from its own speakers. A Mac host's output is muted (the next stream, or a volume key, unmutes it). The PC's outputs are muted too, and its players paused where they can be asked to (MPRIS: a browser's video, most music players), unless a stream starts again within four seconds, as it does when its window is resized.

## A Linux PC (Hyprland)

- **A display when there is no monitor.** Booted headless, Hyprland has no output and Sunshine finds no encoder. `pc/sunshine-virtual-display` makes a virtual output when Sunshine starts.
- **A display that follows the window.** The same script, as the prep command of Sunshine's Desktop app, sets that output to the size the stream asks for.
- **A display that stays that size.** Every time Hyprland rereads its configuration (a theme change, a scaling change, an edit) the virtual output falls back to 1080p and the stream would be stretched from it. `pc/sunshine-virtual-display-watch.service` puts the stream's size back, and keeps a scale you chose on the PC.
- **Awake and unlocked.** A blanked or locked PC streams black or its lock screen. `mac/pc-wake.sh`, as the **Before connecting** command, turns the output on, unlocks the session, and prints the PC's address on the home network when your Mac can reach it there. It is written for one PC: give it yours (`REMOTE_PC_NAME`, and the user name in its ssh line), with ssh that asks no password. Its unlocking needs `pc/sudoers-99-kvm-unlock` installed on the PC for your user; `pc/install.sh` does not do that, nor open the firewall.

- **The instant pointer, with the cursor's shapes.** Your Mac draws the pointer, which moves with your hand, and the PC keeps its own cursor out of the picture: `mac/pc-wake.sh` tells the PC so when the device's **Instant pointer** is on. Wayland tells nobody what the cursor looks like, so the PC goes about it sideways, with nothing added to Hyprland. For the length of the stream its session uses a cursor theme whose cursors are clear pictures, each of a width of its own (`pc/ghost-theme.py`): the cursor is there, and there is nothing of it to see. A small helper (`pc/cursor-share.c`, started by your Mac over ssh, so the device needs its **Clipboard** destination) hears the size of the cursor's picture whenever it changes, by a standard capture protocol, and sends your Mac that shape as the PC's usual theme draws it. `pc/install.sh` builds both. A cursor that an application draws for itself is not one of the theme's and shows in the picture as it is. Without the helper, or without a Clipboard destination, the PC hides its cursor for the stream and your pointer is a plain arrow. The app's undo command (`sunshine-virtual-display release`) puts the PC's cursor back as it was when the stream ends, and Sunshine starting does too.

- **Encoders tried once.** Sunshine 2026.516 on Linux tests every encoder again each time a stream is launched, which with NVENC is 1.3 s of every connect and every window resize. `pc/sunshine-probe-once/setup.sh` and then `build.sh`, run on the PC, build that same version with the one line changed (no root needed), and `pc/install.sh` after them puts it in service; newer Sunshine no longer needs it.

With an NVIDIA card stuck on an older driver, check that your Sunshine build's encoder still matches it (a GTX 1080 on driver 580 needs Sunshine 2026.516).

`pc/install.sh`, run on the PC, puts the configuration, the script and the two services in place. `pc/doctor.sh` checks all of this from another machine, including that what is installed is what is in the repository.
